# Plain-language explainer

For a reader with no background. `WRITEUP.md` is the technical version.

## The object in one sentence

A single unbroken line that passes through every tile of a pattern that never repeats, visiting each tile once,
generated by a small recursive recipe and proven to work at every scale.

## The two ingredients

- **The spectre** is a single shape, found in 2023, that covers the infinite plane with no gaps but only in patterns
  that never repeat. Mathematicians looked for such a shape for about 60 years.
- **A space-filling curve**, like Hilbert's from 1891, threads through every cell of a square grid while keeping cells
  that are close on the line close in the plane. Databases and image formats use this to flatten 2D data into 1D.

## The question this object answers

The spectre answers: is there one shape that tiles the plane but only without repeating?

This object answers the next question:

> Can you walk through the whole tiling one tile at a time, always stepping to a tile that touches the one you are on,
> visiting every tile exactly once, and never returning to a region after you have left it?

Yes, and a fixed recipe of 30 routes produces the walk at any size.

Said another way: Hilbert threaded a square grid with one line. Does that depend on the grid being regular? Here it
still works on a pattern with no repetition at all.

"Never returning to a region" is the part that matters. Without it you are asking for some path through every tile.
With it you are asking for a path that respects the nested structure of the tiling at every scale, which is the
property that makes Hilbert's curve useful.

## How it is built

It is the same move as the Hilbert animation people have seen: draw a small route, make copies, join them, repeat.

- **The tiling groups itself.** Spectre tiles cluster in groups of 8 (occasionally 7). Those groups cluster into bigger
  groups of 8, and so on forever. Each bigger group is a rougher, larger cousin of the smaller one.
- **The motif is a route through one group.** "Enter here, visit the 8 pieces in this order, leave there." To grow the
  curve, replace each piece with its own route. The ends line up, so they join into one line.
- **Thirty routes.** Hilbert needs one route in four rotations. The spectre groups have no symmetry and the ends meet in
  awkward places, so it takes 30. Each route says which of the 30 to use inside each of its 8 pieces.

The page in `art/spectre-thread.html` plays this: one tile, 9, 71, 559, 4,401.

## Non-periodic versus aperiodic

A space-filling curve that never repeats is old news. Hilbert's own curve never repeats: the grid under it does, the
line does not. But that is a choice. The same grid can be threaded by a plain back-and-forth snake that repeats.

Two words keep these apart:

- **Non-periodic:** it happens not to repeat.
- **Aperiodic:** it cannot repeat, because the pieces rule it out.

Every piece of this thread sits in the same cell shape, and that shape cannot be arranged periodically. No thread
through these cells could repeat, however it was routed. The spectre turns "doesn't repeat" into "can't repeat".

A ladder of versions of the question:

1. A curve that happens not to repeat. Known since Hilbert.
2. A curve on a forced-non-repeating tiling with two tile shapes. Done in 2014 on the Ammann A5 tiles.
3. A curve on a forced-non-repeating tiling with one tile shape, with a proof. This project. Novelty unconfirmed.
4. One tile with a mark on it, such that fitting copies together forces one unbroken line. Ruled out on the spectre
   in its direct forms (see below).

## Order without symmetry

- A **spiral** covers a page with one uncrossed line too, but it is completely regular: one formula, and far from the
  centre it looks like evenly spaced parallel lines.
- A **random scribble** has no symmetry and no rule.
- **This thread** has no translation symmetry and no mirror symmetry, yet it is fully determined by 30 route patterns.
  Any stretch of it shows up again and again elsewhere, never far away, and the copies never fall into a grid.

Order without symmetry is what made aperiodic tilings and quasicrystals surprising. This is a line that carries that
property through a two-dimensional pattern, while staying local the way Hilbert's curve does.

## Is it a fractal?

In structure, yes.

- It is built by one rule applied at every scale.
- At any finite size it is an ordinary line. In the limit it comes arbitrarily close to every point of the region.
- The outlines of the clusters are ragged at every scale. From our counts, boundary tiles grow about 4.25 times per
  round while width grows about 2.8 times, which gives an outline dimension of roughly 1.4. This is an estimate.

It is never an exact copy of itself. Each larger cluster is a rougher cousin of the smaller one. The technical name
for this kind of object is a graph-directed fractal: several shapes each built from smaller versions of one another.

## How good is the curve?

Locality measures how far apart two tiles can be in the plane compared with how far apart they are along the line.
Hilbert's curve scores 6. The 30-route curve scores 8.98. A short search found curves scoring 8.77 and 8.75 at the
cost of more routes (122 and 181). The best possible value is unknown.

## Can one marked tile draw it by itself?

Tested, and the direct forms fail on the spectre.

- **One stroke per tile** (in by one edge, out by another) cannot be consistent: each edge of a spectre meets three to
  five different edges of its neighbours.
- **Seven arcs per tile**, pairing up all 14 edges: there are 429 ways without crossings, and every one produces
  closed loops, so none gives a single line.

What does work is a small palette: the same tile with **20 different strokes** is enough to draw this curve, and 20 is
the minimum for this particular curve. Other curves might need fewer. Drawn the natural way, through each tile's centre
and the middle of each shared border, the curve uses 32 strokes; those are the ones shown on the page.

## A form that fits in one picture: the spectre tree

The 30 routes are needed because a path has two ends that must land in the right places. A tree has no ends.

- The 8 pieces of every cluster sit in a ring. Each piece touches the next at one shared corner.
- At that corner, one tile from each piece meets the other along an edge. Call that a door. This holds at every size.
- Open every door except one, so the ring becomes a chain. Do the same inside each piece, and inside those.
- This grows a tree through all the tiles. Walk around the tree, keeping a hand on it, and you trace one closed loop
  that never crosses itself and passes every tile.

The whole recipe is "join neighbouring pieces at the corner they share, and repeat". The price: the loop passes some
tiles two or three times, and it is about half as tidy as the 30-route line. `results/spectre_tree.png` shows it.

## So what?

Nothing in daily life changes. The fair answers:

1. It answers a natural question about a famous new shape.
2. You can make things with it: a plotter, embroidery machine, laser cutter or 3D printer can draw an endless
   non-repeating design in one stroke.
3. It may help people who simulate quasicrystals, who often thread a 2D material into a 1D chain. Untried.
4. The proof method, reducing infinitely many sizes to a few dozen checkable cases, should carry to other tilings.
5. It went from a vague idea to a machine-checked result in about a day of conversation with an AI.

## Status

Computer-assisted and unreviewed. General theory for such curves exists. A short search found no version for the
spectre. Whether it is new is a question for an expert.
